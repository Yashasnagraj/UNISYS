1. Executive Introduction: The Paradigm Shift to 
Functional Mechanics 
The landscape of medical diagnostics is undergoing a fundamental transformation, shifting from 
static imaging to dynamic functional characterization. While traditional modalities such as X-ray, 
Computed Tomography (CT), and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) excel at visualizing 
anatomical structure, they often fail to capture the mechanical properties of tissue—stiffness, 
elasticity, tone, and resonance—which are frequently the earliest biomarkers of pathology. The 
user’s proposal to develop a device based on Active Mechanical Excitation (AME) sits at the 
forefront of this shift, leveraging the principle that a physical system’s response to a mechanical 
impulse reveals its intrinsic health status. 
This report provides an exhaustive analysis of the current commercial and research landscape 
for devices operating on similar principles. By examining established technologies like 
MyotonPRO, Osstell ISQ, and FibroScan, alongside emerging solutions for fracture and 
ascites detection, we validate the scientific feasibility and market viability of the proposed 
device. The analysis distinguishes between passive diagnostics (which listen to internal sounds, 
like a stethoscope) and active diagnostics (which inject energy to elicit a response), confirming 
that the latter offers superior quantification and objectivity. 
The report identifies a critical "missing middle" in the market: while specialized devices exist for 
liver fibrosis and dental implants, there is a distinct lack of a general-purpose, handheld 
mechanical diagnostic tool for point-of-care assessment of abdominal pathology (ascites, 
pneumothorax) and long-bone integrity. This gap represents a significant strategic opportunity 
for the proposed device. 
1.1 Principles of Active Mechanical Excitation 
To understand the competitive landscape, it is essential to define the physics governing these 
devices. Unlike passive auscultation, AME devices treat biological tissue as a complex 
mechanical system defined by mass, stiffness, and damping. 
1.1.1 The Mass-Spring-Damper Model 
Most devices in this category model tissue response using the Kelvin-Voigt model of 
viscoelasticity. When a mechanical impulse (a tap or vibration) is applied to tissue, the tissue 
resists deformation through stiffness (acting like a spring) and dissipates energy through 
viscosity (acting like a hydraulic damper). 
● Stiffness (k): Correlates with fibrosis in livers or contraction in muscles. 
● Damping (c): Correlates with fluid content (edema) or tissue composition. 
● Mass (m): Correlates with the volume of tissue being mobilized. 
1.1.2 Resonance Frequency Analysis (RFA) 
RFA relies on the phenomenon where an object vibrates at a specific "natural frequency" 
determined by its stiffness and boundary conditions. In medical applications, a stiffer interface 
(e.g., a well-healed bone fracture or a solid dental implant) yields a higher resonance frequency. 
This principle allows for the non-invasive assessment of structural integrity without radiation. 
1.1.3 Shear Wave Propagation 
Used in elastography, this method generates a transverse wave (ripple) through the tissue. The 
speed of this wave (V_s) is directly related to the shear modulus (G) of the tissue, where \mu = 
\rho V_s^2. Stiffer tissues propagate waves faster. This principle has revolutionized hepatology 
by replacing biopsies. 
2. The Soft Tissue Specialist: MyotonPRO and Digital 
Palpation 
The MyotonPRO (Myoton AS) represents the most direct analog to a handheld 
general-purpose mechanical diagnostic tool, though its current commercial indication is 
restricted to superficial muscles and tendons. It serves as a primary proof-of-concept for the 
user’s proposal regarding the digitization of manual palpation. 
2.1 Operating Principle: The Mechanical Impulse 
The MyotonPRO operates on the principle of Digital Palpation or the Mechanical Dynamic 
Response method. Unlike manual palpation, which is subjective and dependent on the 
physician's tactile sensitivity, MyotonPRO delivers a standardized mechanical impulse to the 
skin surface. 
● Mechanism: The device probe pre-compresses the subcutaneous tissue (Preload: 0.18 
N) to ensure good contact. It then releases a short mechanical impulse (Force: 0.4 N; 
Duration: 15 ms). 
● Sensing: An accelerometer detects the subsequent damped oscillation of the tissue. 
● Data Processing: The device performs a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) on the 
acceleration signal to derive viscoelastic parameters.

● Inter-Operator Reliability: Studies involving athletes (footballers, MMA fighters)

2.4 Market Positioning and Relevance to Proposal 
● Positives: The MyotonPRO validates that a handheld, non-invasive device can quantify 
tissue properties in seconds. It is cost-effective compared to imaging (~$6,000 range) and 
requires no consumables. 
● Gap/Opportunity: The MyotonPRO is marketed primarily for research and sports 
medicine. It is not utilized for internal medicine diagnostics (e.g., organomegaly, masses, 
fluid collections). This leaves the entire vertical of clinical internal medicine open for a 
similar handheld mechanical device. 
3. The Hard Tissue Specialist: Osstell and Resonance 
Frequency 
While Myoton handles soft tissue, Osstell (W&H Dentalwerk) dominates the market for hard 
tissue interfaces, specifically dental implants. It provides a blueprint for how to commercialize 
Resonance Frequency Analysis (RFA). 
3.1 Operating Principle: Magnetic Pulse Excitation 
Osstell does not rely on direct physical tapping of the bone, which can be painful or damaging. 
Instead, it utilizes an intermediate transducer. 
● The SmartPeg: A small, precision-engineered aluminum peg with a magnet is screwed 
into the dental implant. 
● Excitation: The handheld Osstell Beacon probe emits magnetic pulses that cause the 
SmartPeg to vibrate without physical contact. 
● Analysis: The device listens for the resonance frequency of the peg. As the implant 
integrates with the bone (osseointegration), the interface becomes stiffer, and the 
resonance frequency rises. 
3.2 The ISQ Scale: Standardization of Data 
A critical success factor for Osstell—and a key recommendation for the user’s proposal—is the 
conversion of raw physics data (Hertz) into a user-friendly clinical index. 
● ISQ (Implant Stability Quotient): A scale from 1 to 100. 
○ < 60 (Red): Low stability. Risk of failure. 
○ 60-70 (Yellow): Medium stability. 
○ > 70 (Green): High stability. Safe for immediate loading (attaching the tooth). 
● Strategic Value: This scale allows dentists to make binary decisions (Load/Wait) without 
needing to understand the underlying physics of resonance frequencies (typically 
3000–8000 Hz). 
3.3 Clinical Guidelines and Market Adoption 
The device is integrated into clinical workflows. Guidelines suggest measuring ISQ at placement 
(baseline) and again before final restoration. A drop in ISQ alerts the clinician to failure before it 
is visible on X-ray. 
● Accuracy: RFA is shown to be more sensitive than rotational torque tests and avoids the 
damage caused by torque testing. Studies show a strong correlation between ISQ values 
and lateral micro-mobility. 
3.4 Commercial Model: The "Razor and Blade" Strategy 
Osstell’s financial model is highly relevant to the user's proposal. 
● Hardware: The handheld Osstell Beacon retails for approximately $3,500 – $4,000 
USD. 
● Consumables: The SmartPegs are single-patient use devices (approx. $20–$30 per 
pack). Because the measurement depends on the precise mass of the peg, users cannot 
reuse them accurately (wear and tear alters the frequency), ensuring a recurring revenue 
stream. 
● Takeaway for Proposal: If the proposed device can utilize a disposable tip or coupling 
element, it can secure long-term revenue beyond the initial hardware sale. 
4. The Deep Organ Specialist: FibroScan and 
Elastography 
To assess deep visceral organs (specifically the liver), simple surface tapping is insufficient. 
FibroScan (Echosens) solved this by combining mechanical excitation with ultrasonic tracking. 
4.1 Operating Principle: Vibration-Controlled Transient Elastography 
(VCTE) 
FibroScan uses a specialized probe containing both a mechanical vibrator and an ultrasound 
transducer. 
1. Excitation: The vibrator hits the skin, sending a low-frequency (50 Hz) shear wave into 
the liver. 
2. Tracking: The ultrasound transducer emits pulses to track the speed of the shear wave 
as it travels through the liver tissue. 
3. Calculation: Stiffness (E) is calculated from velocity (V) using E = 3 \rho V^2. The result 
is displayed in kilopascals (kPa). 
4.2 Clinical Impact: Replacing Biopsy 
FibroScan demonstrates the immense value of mechanical diagnostics. Before this device, 
staging liver fibrosis required a painful, invasive needle biopsy with risks of bleeding. FibroScan 
turned this into a 5-minute painless office procedure. 
● Positives for Proposal: It proves that the medical market places a high premium on 
non-invasive mechanical quantification. Hospitals are willing to pay significantly for 
devices that reduce invasive procedures. 
4.3 Limitations as Opportunities 
Despite its success, FibroScan has specific limitations that the user’s proposed device could 
address: 
● Ascites Failure: VCTE fails in patients with ascites (abdominal fluid) because shear 
waves do not propagate through fluids. This leads to measurement failure or unreliable 
results. 
○ Implication for Proposal: A device based on acoustic resonance or percussion 
(rather than shear wave) would specifically excel at detecting ascites, as fluid 
creates a distinct resonant signature (dullness) compared to solid tissue. This 
creates a complementary market niche. 
● Obesity: Signal attenuation in obese patients is a challenge, requiring specialized "XL" 
probes. 
● Cost: FibroScan is capital equipment, costing tens of thousands of dollars ($12,000 - 
$45,000+ depending on model and region). A handheld screener at a lower price point 
would be disruptive. 
5. The Missing Middle: Percussion, Fracture, and 
Pneumothorax 
The analysis reveals a "Missing Middle" in the current device landscape. We have superficial 
muscle tools (Myoton) and deep liver tools (FibroScan), but no widely used handheld device for 
general abdominal percussion or fracture screening. This is the most fertile ground for the 
user's proposal. 
5.1 The "Bessman Percussor" and the Failure of Analog Aids 
Historically, devices like the Bessman Percussor attempted to aid physical exams. This device 
was a mechanical tapper that clipped onto a stethoscope. 
● Failure Mode: It remained an analog tool. It standardized the "tap" but still relied on the 
physician's ear to interpret the sound. It did not solve the subjectivity problem. 
● Lesson for Proposal: To succeed, the proposed device must be fully digital. It must 
both deliver the impulse and analyze the response, providing a binary or scalar output 
(e.g., "Fluid Detected" or "Fracture Probability: High") rather than just amplifying the 
sound. 
5.2 Abdominal Ascites and Pneumothorax Detection 
Manual percussion is used to detect shifting dullness (ascites) or hyper-resonance 
(pneumothorax). However, manual sensitivity is only ~83% and specificity ~56%. 
● Research Validation: Studies using digital accelerometers and "mobile percussographs" 
have shown that automated analysis can achieve accuracies >90% in classifying chest 
and abdominal sounds. These systems analyze the signal's frequency spectrum (FFT) 
and decay rate. 
● Commercial Void: While startups like Nilocas Ltd and Tabla are exploring acoustic 
detection for specific conditions (coronary disease, pneumonia), there is no dominant 
"digital pleximeter" in the market. Sequana Medical has commercialized a pump 
(alfapump) for treating ascites, identifying a market of 70,000+ refractory patients, but 
they do not offer a handheld diagnostic tool. This represents a clear entry point for the 
proposal. 
5.3 Fracture Detection: The "Tuning Fork" Gap 
In pre-hospital settings (ambulances, sports fields), diagnosing a fracture without X-ray is 
difficult. 
● Current Method: Tuning forks (128 Hz) are used. The vibrating fork is placed on the 
bone distal to the injury. If the patient feels pain, or if the sound transmission is reduced 
(auscultation), a fracture is suspected. 
● Limitations: Sensitivity is decent (75-92%) but specificity is poor (18-94%). Pain is 
subjective. 
● Technological Validation: Emerging research (e.g., University of Waterloo) uses 
microwave antennas or electromagnetic coupling to detect fractures. However, a simpler 
mechanical resonance device (similar to Osstell but for long bones) could detect the 
"stiffness drop" caused by a fracture breaks the bone's structural continuity. 
● Commercial Void: Currently, the market offers simple tuning forks ($15) or expensive 
bone growth stimulators ($5,000+). There is no diagnostic device in the middle. 
6. Strategic Validation: Points that Stand for the 
Proposal 
Based on the synthesis of the devices above, the following strategic points directly support the 
user's proposal for a new active mechanical diagnostic device. 
6.1 Objective Quantification of Subjective Exams 
● Point: The proposal replaces the "Art of Medicine" with the "Science of Measurement." 
● Evidence: Manual percussion and palpation are subjective and dying skills. MyotonPRO 
proves that digital sensors can achieve reliability (ICC > 0.90) that human hands cannot 
match. The proposal creates a standardized record of the exam, which is crucial for 
Electronic Health Records (EHR) and malpractice defense. 
6.2 The "Traffic Light" Triage Capability 
● Point: The device can offer rapid, binary triage decisions for non-experts (nurses, 
paramedics). 
● Evidence: Just as Osstell gives a Green/Red light for dental loading , the proposed 
device could give a "Red Light" for Pneumothorax or Fracture. This democratizes 
diagnostic capability, allowing lower-level providers to make critical decisions in the field. 
6.3 Filling the "Fluid Gap" in Ultrasound 
● Point: The device succeeds where Ultrasound fails. 
● Evidence: FibroScan and standard Ultrasound struggle with obesity and signal 
attenuation in ascites. A mechanical percussion device operating in the audible range is 
less susceptible to these specific limitations. By detecting the resonance of fluid (which is 
distinct from solid tissue), the device turns a competitor's weakness into its own strength. 
6.4 Longitudinal Monitoring of Healing 
● Point: The device enables safe, frequent monitoring of fracture healing or muscle 
recovery. 
● Evidence: X-rays show structure (calcium) but not stiffness (strength). A fracture might 
look healed on X-ray but still be mechanically weak. Conversely, it might be strong 
enough to bear weight before the X-ray looks perfect. A mechanical resonance device 
(like Osstell for femurs) provides the functional data needed to clear an athlete to play or 
a senior to walk, without repeated radiation exposure. 
6.5 Cost-Effective Accessibility 
● Point: The device provides high-value diagnostics at a fraction of the cost of imaging. 
● Evidence: With a target price range of $1,000 - $5,000 (validated by Osstell/Myoton 
pricing), the device is accessible to urgent care centers, ambulances, and sports teams 
that cannot afford CT scanners. This fits the global trend toward point-of-care (POC) 
diagnostics.

8. Engineering and Implementation Challenges 
To demonstrate a nuanced understanding, the report must acknowledge the technical hurdles 
the proposal will face, as identified in the research literature. 
8.1 The Coupling Problem 
● Issue: Transmitting mechanical energy through the skin (soft, damping) to measure a 
deep organ or bone is difficult. The skin acts as a low-pass filter and a damper. 
● Research Insight: Myoton measurements are affected by skin thickness. 
● Solution: The proposal should incorporate Multi-Modal Sensing. Combining the 
mechanical actuator with both an accelerometer (to measure surface recoil) and a 
microphone (to measure acoustic resonance) can help isolate the deep tissue signal from 
the surface noise. Low-frequency excitation (< 1000 Hz) penetrates better than high 
frequency. 
8.2 Standardization of Force 
● Issue: To compare readings over time, the "tap" must be identical every time. 
● Research Insight: Myoton uses a solenoid with a pre-load mechanism to ensure 
constant force (0.4 N) regardless of how hard the operator presses. 
● Solution: The proposed device must use an electromechanical actuator (solenoid or 
voice coil) rather than a spring-loaded manual hammer to ensure digital reproducibility. 
8.3 Biological Variability 
● Issue: "Normal" stiffness varies by age, BMI, and hydration. 
● Research Insight: There is no single "normal" frequency for a human femur or abdomen. 
● Solution: The device should focus on Asymmetry (comparing left vs. right leg) or 
Longitudinal Change (monitoring the same patient over days). This removes the need 
for a universal absolute baseline. 
9. Start-up and Competitor Landscape 
The landscape is active but not crowded in the specific niche of the proposal. 
● Sequana Medical: Focused on treating ascites with the alfapump, an implantable pump. 
Their market data (70,000 refractory patients) validates the massive need for better 
ascites management, supporting the business case for a diagnostic screener. 
● Nilocas Ltd: Developing a chest pad for coronary heart disease detection using acoustic 
analysis of turbulent blood flow. This confirms the trend toward acoustic/mechanical 
diagnostics. 
● ElectroSonics Medical / LLNL: Developed a "pneumothorax detector" using 
ultra-wideband (UWB) radar. This is a competitor using a different physics principle (RF 
vs. Mechanical), highlighting the demand for pneumothorax screening. 
● Brain-Computer Interface / Neurotech: Startups like NeoAcoustic are using ultrasound 
for brain stimulation, showing the versatility of acoustic technologies, though less relevant 
to the direct proposal. 
10. Conclusion 
The search results confirm that the user's proposed device operates on a scientifically valid 
and commercially proven principle. The success of Osstell in dentistry and FibroScan in 
hepatology demonstrates that the medical market values non-invasive mechanical quantification 
of tissue properties and is willing to pay premium prices for it. 
The proposal stands to succeed by targeting the "Missing Middle"—the detection of 
abdominal fluid/air (ascites/pneumothorax) and long-bone fractures. These are 
high-volume, critical conditions currently managed by subjective manual exams or expensive 
imaging. 
Key Positives for the Proposal: 
1. Clinical Objectivity: Converting subjective "dullness" or "pain" into objective digital 
metrics (Stiffness Index, Resonance Score). 
2. Safety Profile: Zero radiation and non-invasive nature allow for unlimited repeat testing 
(monitoring). 
3. Market Gap: No dominant handheld competitor exists for general abdominal percussion 
or fracture screening. 
4. Cost Efficiency: Positioning as a cost-effective alternative to CT/X-ray for rapid triage. 
By leveraging the engineering precedents of uniform impulse delivery (Myoton) and simplified 
index output (Osstell), the proposed device can redefine the standard of care for physical 
examinations.
